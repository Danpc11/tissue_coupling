Circadian phase and amplitude from a single transcriptome

Companion tool for Independent hormonal and circadian signals coordinate human metabolic tissues. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.

What this does. Given an expression matrix of clock genes across individuals, it orders the samples around a cycle, returning each individual's relative phase and the amplitude of their rhythm. With two tissues from the same people, it measures how coupled their phases are.

What it cannot do. The ordering defines phase only up to rotation and reflection, so absolute clock time is not recoverable. Values are not comparable between datasets with different sampling windows. This is a pseudo-quantification of phase.

1. Paste an expression matrix

Tab- or comma-separated. First row: sample names. First column: gene symbols. Values on any log scale. Include as many clock genes as you have — BMAL1 (or ARNTL) and DBP are required for the validation check.

2. Second tissue, same individuals (optional)

Sample names must match those of the first matrix. Only shared samples are used.

Method

The two leading components of the clock-gene matrix define an initial angle per sample, refined iteratively against a cosinor waveform fitted to the dataset itself. Amplitude is the norm of each individual's projection onto the orthonormal basis of that waveform, which makes it independent of phase. Coupling between tissues uses the absolute Jammalamadaka–Sarma circular correlation, invariant to rotation and reflection, with a permutation P computed within the dataset.

A validation step checks that the recovered cycle is the clock and not merely the dominant cyclic axis of that tissue: BMAL1 and DBP must fall in antiphase, 8–16 h apart. In our analysis whole blood, hypothalamus and cerebral cortex failed this check. Without it, blood would have been reported as the best-coupled tissue, which is the opposite of what it is.
